Action Learning for Scientific Simulations

Sarah Liaw1,2, Yuanqi Du3, Yilun Du1,2
1Kempner Institute 2Harvard University 3Microsoft Research New England
Paper Code

TLDR: We introduce action learning, which treats the stochastic action of a system as the learning target and recovers it from trajectory samples as a differentiable path space objective, reusable for inference, steering and sampling without retraining.

Abstract

Understanding and controlling how systems evolve is central to science and engineering, where many problems require reasoning about complete trajectories. Existing approaches typically learn local dynamics or generate trajectories, but objectives constructed from individual transitions can miss dependencies across time, while generative models do not necessarily provide a tractable objective for evaluating and optimizing arbitrary paths. We study action learning, the problem of recovering a stochastic action from trajectory observations, yielding a common objective for reasoning about dynamical systems. We introduce LEAP (Learned Energy on Action Paths), which anchors the unknown action to a tractable reference process and learns the remaining correction by comparing observed trajectories with reference trajectories. This construction yields a differentiable objective on path space that can capture both local dynamical structure and nonadditive dependencies across time. Once learned, the same action can be flexibly reused for path ranking and inference, mechanism steering, constrained optimization and sampling. Across stochastic physical systems, many-body orbital dynamics and molecular simulations, LEAP recovers underlying dynamics, captures dependencies across time and enables transfer across inference objectives and physical conditions. We show that action learning is a promising framework for turning trajectory observations into reusable objectives for scientific inference and control.

From Trajectory Samples to Path Inference

Understanding how systems move between states requires reasoning about the trajectories that connect them, whether two molecular conformations are joined by several transition routes through different intermediates or a robot acts through a sequence of decisions. In these settings the object of interest is often the trajectory itself, namely which paths realize a transition, which mechanisms are plausible and which paths remain likely under perturbations or constraints, and answering such questions requires an objective on complete paths. When the dynamics are known, stochastic action functionals such as the Onsager–Machlup functional provide such an objective, assigning to every complete trajectory a physical cost whose main term, for a diffusion, accumulates how far the velocity departs from the drift.

In many applications, however, the action is unknown and we observe trajectories from simulation or experiment, such as molecular simulation traces, robot trajectories, policy rollouts or logs of a stochastic process. Learned methods typically fit a one step predictor or a trajectory generator to such data, while downstream trajectory inference calls for a reusable objective on complete paths. We call the task of recovering an evaluable action from trajectory samples action learning, and the central idea of this work is to treat the action itself as the object to learn, so that one learned action can rank, optimize, steer and sample paths long after training.

Learning Action Functionals from Trajectory Samples

Main Idea

LEAP learns a differentiable stochastic action from trajectory samples and a reference process, without access to the drift, the potential or the simulator likelihood. Our approach makes the following observations about a discretized trajectory \(\Phi = (x_0, x_1, \dots, x_N)\) at times \(t_k = k\Delta t\), observed under a context \(c\) such as endpoints, a terminal region or a horizon.

  1. Following Onsager–Machlup and Freidlin–Wentzell theory, we take the action of a trajectory to be its negative log path density, so lower action means higher probability and the minimizers are the most probable paths,
    \[ S^*(\Phi;c) \;=\; -\log p_{\mathrm{data}}(\Phi \mid c) \;+\; \alpha(c), \]
    where \(\alpha(c)\) is constant across paths with the same context.
  2. A reference process such as Brownian motion has a path density we can evaluate and sample, so with \(S_{\mathrm{ref}} = -\log p_{\mathrm{ref}}\) the unknown action splits into a known part and a path log ratio,
    \[ S^*(\Phi;c) \;=\; \underbrace{S_{\mathrm{ref}}(\Phi;c)}_{\text{reference action}} \;-\; \underbrace{G^*(\Phi;c)}_{\log p_{\mathrm{data}}/p_{\mathrm{ref}}} \;+\; \alpha(c). \]
  3. Up to a constant per context, a path log ratio is what the optimal classifier computes when it picks out the observed trajectory from reference trajectories of the same context.

Then, at a high level, LEAP draws one observed trajectory \(\Phi^+ \sim p_{\mathrm{data}}(\cdot \mid c)\) and \(K\) reference trajectories \(\Phi^-_k \sim p_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot \mid c)\) and minimizes the setwise comparison objective

\[ \mathcal{L}_K(\theta) \;=\; -\,\mathbb{E}\left[\log \frac{e^{G_\theta(\Phi^+;c)}}{e^{G_\theta(\Phi^+;c)} + \sum_{k=1}^{K} e^{G_\theta(\Phi^-_k;c)}}\right]. \]

When the candidates are drawn independently, the data and reference densities share their support and the model class contains \(G^*\), the objective is minimized exactly at \(G_\theta = G^* + a(c)\) for every \(K \ge 1\), and at this optimum \(S_{\mathrm{ref}} - G_\theta\) ranks, optimizes and samples complete trajectories within each context as the data action does. Two properties of trajectory data shape how we choose the reference and parameterize \(G_\theta\).

  • Trajectories are often selected by an event, such as reaching a target region, which reshapes every transition. We address this with matched selection and the Girsanov form.
  • Some information persists along the whole path, such as a hidden physical parameter, which couples all transitions. We address this with a global path functional.

Learning on Paths

The comparison objective recovers the action because of what its optimal classifier computes. When the observed trajectory sits at a uniformly random index \(I\) among the \(K+1\) candidates, Bayes' rule gives a softmax over the true path log ratios,

\[ P(I = i \mid \Phi_{0:K}, c) \;=\; \frac{p_{\mathrm{data}}(\Phi_i \mid c)/p_{\mathrm{ref}}(\Phi_i \mid c)}{\sum_{\ell=0}^{K} p_{\mathrm{data}}(\Phi_\ell \mid c)/p_{\mathrm{ref}}(\Phi_\ell \mid c)} \;=\; \frac{e^{G^*(\Phi_i;c)}}{\sum_{\ell=0}^{K} e^{G^*(\Phi_\ell;c)}}. \]
\[ \begin{aligned} P(I = i \mid \Phi_{0:K}, c) &= \frac{p_{\mathrm{data}}(\Phi_i \mid c)/p_{\mathrm{ref}}(\Phi_i \mid c)}{\sum_{\ell=0}^{K} p_{\mathrm{data}}(\Phi_\ell \mid c)/p_{\mathrm{ref}}(\Phi_\ell \mid c)} \\ &= \frac{e^{G^*(\Phi_i;c)}}{\sum_{\ell=0}^{K} e^{G^*(\Phi_\ell;c)}}. \end{aligned} \]

The objective is minimized when the model's softmax matches this posterior, which makes every difference of \(G_\theta\) between two paths of a context equal that of \(G^*\) and leaves one free constant per context. Centering under the reference fixes that constant, and at the optimum the learned action

\[ S_\theta(\Phi;c) \;=\; -\log p_{\mathrm{ref}}(\Phi \mid c) \;-\; \tilde G_\theta(\Phi;c), \qquad \tilde G_\theta(\Phi;c) \;=\; G_\theta(\Phi;c) - \mathbb{E}_{\Phi' \sim p_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot \mid c)}\big[G_\theta(\Phi';c)\big], \]
\[ \begin{aligned} S_\theta(\Phi;c) &= -\log p_{\mathrm{ref}}(\Phi \mid c) - \tilde G_\theta(\Phi;c), \\ \tilde G_\theta(\Phi;c) &= G_\theta(\Phi;c) - \mathbb{E}_{\Phi' \sim p_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot \mid c)}\big[G_\theta(\Phi';c)\big], \end{aligned} \]

equals \(S^*\) up to a constant per context, which leaves path rankings, optimization solutions and Metropolis–Hastings ratios unchanged.

The Girsanov Form

The objective leaves the form of \(G_\theta\) open, and for Markov dynamics the reference suggests one. We use a Gaussian Markov reference, \(x_{k+1} = x_k + b_{\mathrm{ref}}(x_k,t_k;c)\,\Delta t + \sqrt{2D\Delta t}\,\xi_k\) with \(\xi_k \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)\), whose transition law is analytic. When the data also follow Euler–Maruyama dynamics with the same diffusion \(D\), initial law and time grid, differing only in drift, \(b_{\mathrm{data}} = b_{\mathrm{ref}} + u^*\), LEAP parameterizes the path log ratio in its Girsanov form, with \(v_k = (x_{k+1} - x_k)/\Delta t\) and \(u_\theta\) parameterizing the drift difference,

\[ G_\theta(\Phi;c) \;=\; \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} \Big[\underbrace{\tfrac{1}{2D}\,u_\theta(x_k,t_k;c)^{\top}\big(v_k - b_{\mathrm{ref}}(x_k,t_k;c)\big)}_{\text{velocity alignment}} \;-\; \underbrace{\tfrac{1}{4D}\,\|u_\theta(x_k,t_k;c)\|^2}_{\text{normalization cost}}\Big]\,\Delta t. \]
\[ \begin{aligned} G_\theta(\Phi;c) = \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} \Big[ &\tfrac{1}{2D}\,u_\theta(x_k,t_k;c)^{\top}\big(v_k - b_{\mathrm{ref}}(x_k,t_k;c)\big) \\ &- \tfrac{1}{4D}\,\|u_\theta(x_k,t_k;c)\|^2 \Big]\,\Delta t. \end{aligned} \]

Each term involves one transition, while \(u_\theta\) is fit by comparing complete paths, which is what lets LEAP handle selected trajectories in the first experiment below. At \(u_\theta = u^*\) this is the exact path log likelihood ratio, and for a fixed initial state the data action is, up to a constant \(C_{\mathrm{EM}}(c)\), the Euler–Maruyama action of the data drift,

\[ S_{\mathrm{ref}}(\Phi;c) - G^*(\Phi;c) \;=\; C_{\mathrm{EM}}(c) \;+\; \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} \frac{\Delta t}{4D}\,\big\|v_k - b_{\mathrm{data}}(x_k,t_k;c)\big\|^2. \]
\[ \begin{aligned} S_{\mathrm{ref}}(\Phi;c) - G^*(\Phi;c) &= C_{\mathrm{EM}}(c) \\ &\quad + \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} \frac{\Delta t}{4D}\,\big\|v_k - b_{\mathrm{data}}(x_k,t_k;c)\big\|^2. \end{aligned} \]

Because the path KL divergence between two such processes is a sum of expected squared drift differences along the path, matching the unselected trajectory distribution identifies the drift at every visited state and time. For conservative systems we parameterize the total drift as \(-\nabla U_\theta\), so LEAP also returns a learned potential.

Local and Global Path Functionals

The Girsanov form adds one term per transition, and LEAP-Local more generally learns a scalar term for each transition,

\[ G^{\mathrm{local}}_\theta(\Phi;c) \;=\; \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} g_\theta(x_k, x_{k+1}, t_k; c). \]

This factorization can fail when information is shared across the trajectory, such as a hidden physical parameter fixed for the whole path, so LEAP-Global learns the path log ratio on the complete path, \(G^{\mathrm{global}}_\theta(\Phi;c) = r_\theta(\Phi;c)\). For trajectories selected by an event, such as reaching a target region, we condition the reference on the same event, and the experiments below make both cases precise.

Downstream Trajectory Inference

With the learned action in hand, each downstream question becomes a problem on path space. The same \(S_\theta\) ranks candidate paths, infers representative trajectories, incorporates mechanism preferences, imposes soft constraints and defines trajectory distributions, and preferences \(M_j\) and penalties \(C_\ell\) simply add to it,

\[ \pi(\Phi \mid c) \;\propto\; \exp\Big(-S_\theta(\Phi;c) + \sum_j \gamma_j M_j(\Phi) - \sum_\ell \lambda_\ell C_\ell(\Phi)\Big), \]

so each new request changes the inference problem without retraining the model, and an action within \(\varepsilon\) of the true one up to a constant keeps its minimizers within \(2\varepsilon\) of optimal under the true action.

This only requires trajectory samples and a reference that can be sampled, has an evaluable path density and covers the data, so beyond the physical and molecular systems tested here it can extend to robot trajectories and logged agent rollouts, where a learned action can help analyze and steer behavior in science, robotics and planning.

Recovering Dynamics under Terminal Selection

We first test matched selection. When observed trajectories are kept only if they reach an event \(E\), and the reference is selected by the same event, the complete path log ratio changes only by a constant,

\[ \log \frac{p_{\mathrm{data}}(\Phi \mid E, c)}{p_{\mathrm{ref}}(\Phi \mid E, c)} \;=\; \log \frac{p_{\mathrm{data}}(\Phi \mid c)}{p_{\mathrm{ref}}(\Phi \mid c)} \;+\; \log \frac{P_{\mathrm{ref}}(E \mid c)}{P_{\mathrm{data}}(E \mid c)}, \]
\[ \begin{aligned} \log \frac{p_{\mathrm{data}}(\Phi \mid E, c)}{p_{\mathrm{ref}}(\Phi \mid E, c)} &= \log \frac{p_{\mathrm{data}}(\Phi \mid c)}{p_{\mathrm{ref}}(\Phi \mid c)} \\ &\quad + \log \frac{P_{\mathrm{ref}}(E \mid c)}{P_{\mathrm{data}}(E \mid c)}, \end{aligned} \]

while every one step transition is reshaped. In a double well with drift \(b(x) = x - x^3\), we keep only the paths from the left well that end near the right well. A cubic regression on their steps fits the selected drift even though its basis contains the exact drift (drift MSE 0.7752), while LEAP, with a Brownian reference conditioned on the same window, recovers the original drift (MSE \(3.3 \times 10^{-4}\)). With the learned potential fixed, LEAP also infers near optimal paths to the saddle at new horizons.

Recovering a Nonadditive Trajectory Action

Next, we test the global path functional. An unobserved \(z \in \{-1, +1\}\), drawn once per path, sets the solar mass of a 31 body system to \(m_\odot(1 + z\eta)\). Marginalizing \(z\) gives the exact path log ratio relative to the nominal dynamics,

\[ G^*(\Phi;c) \;=\; \sum_k m_k \;+\; \log\cosh\Big(\sum_k d_k\Big), \]

where \(d_k\) and \(m_k\) combine the per step log likelihoods under \(z = \pm 1\) and the nominal dynamics, and \(A = \sum_k d_k\) is the path summary. The \(\log\cosh\) couples every pair of transitions, so no sum of per step terms can represent it. LEAP-Local, which scores one transition at a time, misses this coupling. LEAP-Global sees the complete path and recovers \(\eta\) as \(1.02 \times 10^{-4}\) against the true \(1.04 \times 10^{-4}\), and the gap persists at 42 bodies and 256 transitions.

Reusing Learned Actions across Conditions and Objectives

Finally, we reuse one learned action across conditions and objectives. We learn one action for alanine dipeptide in its backbone angles \((\phi, \psi)\) from reactive C7eq → C7ax trajectories simulated at 600 K, a reduced description whose effective dynamics are not available in closed form. Keeping this action fixed, we complete paths from a few observed states and add basin preferences and angular constraints without retraining, and with a reference fit to 500 K trajectories, the log ratio learned at 600 K separates 500 K paths from that reference at least as well as a model trained at 500 K.

Conclusion

LEAP treats the stochastic action as a reusable learning target for trajectory data, and learning an objective on path space gives a common interface between trajectory data and diverse downstream inference problems. Looking forward, we hope action learning encourages a broader view for trajectory modeling, in science as well as robotics and other sequential domains, by shifting the focus to learning reusable objectives over complete paths.

Limitations

  • LEAP requires overlap between the reference and the trajectory distribution, and poor overlap increases finite sample error.
  • The action is identified up to a constant within each context, so it ranks and optimizes paths within each context.
  • The log ratio is learned where trajectories go, so inference far from the data relies on extrapolation.
  • The Girsanov form is exact for Euler–Maruyama dynamics that share the diffusion, initial law and time grid, while state dependent diffusion needs more likelihood ratio terms.
  • Projecting onto reduced coordinates can introduce memory and state dependent friction, so the learned action there is an effective one.
